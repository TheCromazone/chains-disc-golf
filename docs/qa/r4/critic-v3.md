## Exactly one largest gap overall

**Outside canopy: flat-looking ground lighting.** B, C, and F devote most of their usable view to a broad, weakly differentiated green surface. The fairway has a bright strip, but little strong light-and-shadow structure to establish its shape and its relationship to the trees. A, D, and especially E have distinct illuminated and shaded ground masses that make the course feel dimensional. At this framing, that difference dominates more screen area than the crown details.

The different courses and camera scales limit direct attribution: this is a judgment of the presented images, not proof that a particular rendering technique is missing. The canopy is not the largest remaining gap in this board.

## Portrait framing check — canopy closure: NO

Inspected `v3-high-tee.png` and `v3-low-tee.png` at their native 430-pixel width, plus `reference-tee.png`. The reference source is 862 pixels wide; the earlier board supplies its 430-pixel presentation.

**The original canopy problem is still visible in both portrait captures.** The nearest broadleaf crown at the left edge has large, smooth, individually readable rounded lobes, including a prominent ball on its right shoulder and a separate flattened lower band. It still looks assembled. Neither quality setting closes that gap. The reference's broadleaf crowns read as continuous shaded masses with smaller, irregular leafy edges.

**Exactly one canopy gap: conspicuous spherical lobe assembly.** To visibly fix it, merge the large lobes into a continuous primary crown, break its edge with much smaller irregular foliage clusters, and carry broad light-to-dark shading across that shared volume. The visible goal is a thick leafy crown whose large component balls can no longer be picked out individually.

The landscape board reduced crown size and increased ground coverage. Its ground-lighting finding does **not** establish canopy completion; using that change in screen area to claim a canopy pass would be a framing artifact.
